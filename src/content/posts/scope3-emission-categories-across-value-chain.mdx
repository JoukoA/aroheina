## Introduction

A company's greenhouse gas emissions extend beyond the activities it directly operates. They are also generated when suppliers extract raw materials, manufacture products, transport goods, provide services, and when customers use or dispose of the products the company sells.

These indirect emissions are known as **Scope 3 emissions**. Under the GHG Protocol, they cover all other indirect emissions that occur in a company's value chain, excluding emissions already accounted for in Scope 2.

To make this broad area manageable, the GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Accounting and Reporting Standard divides Scope 3 into 15 categories. Eight relate to upstream activities and seven to downstream activities.

Understanding these categories is an important first step in building a complete emissions inventory. It helps organisations identify where emissions occur, determine which data they need, and establish where engagement with suppliers, customers and other value-chain partners may be necessary.

## 1. How the 15 categories are organised

The distinction between upstream and downstream emissions is based on the reporting company's value-chain relationships and purchases and sales.

- **Upstream emissions** are associated with goods and services purchased or acquired by the reporting company, as well as other activities supporting its operations.
- **Downstream emissions** are associated with the goods and services the company sells, distributes, leases or otherwise provides, including certain investment activities.

The reporting company sits between these two parts of the value chain. Its own direct emissions are reported under Scope 1, while emissions from purchased electricity, heat, steam and cooling consumed by the company are generally reported under Scope 2.

Scope 3 captures other indirect emissions occurring outside the company's operational boundary.

## 2. Upstream Scope 3 emissions: Categories 1–8

Upstream emissions arise before or in support of a company's own operations. They often involve suppliers, contractors, logistics providers, employees and leased assets.

### Category 1 – Purchased goods and services

This category covers emissions from the extraction, production and transportation of goods and services purchased or acquired by the reporting company during the reporting year.

Examples include:

- Raw materials and components
- Chemicals, lubricants and industrial gases
- Packaging and consumables
- IT services, consultancy and outsourced services
- Maintenance and other contracted services

For energy companies, purchased steel, pipes, valves, compressors, chemicals and drilling services may be relevant sources.

The category can be significant because the production of industrial materials and equipment can generate substantial emissions before those materials reach the purchasing company.

### Category 2 – Capital goods

Capital goods are long-term assets used by a company to produce goods, provide services or support its operations.

Examples include:

- Buildings and industrial facilities
- Production equipment and machinery
- Pipelines and processing equipment
- Vehicles and other major infrastructure
- Drilling rigs and power-generation equipment

The emissions are associated with the extraction, production and transportation of capital goods acquired during the reporting year. They are not the same as the emissions generated when the company operates those assets.

For example, the manufacture of a compressor may contribute to Category 2, while fuel burned to operate it at a company-controlled facility may be Scope 1.

### Category 3 – Fuel- and energy-related activities not included in Scope 1 or Scope 2

This category captures upstream emissions associated with fuels and energy purchased by the reporting company, where those emissions are not already included in Scope 1 or Scope 2.

Examples include:

- Extraction, processing and transportation of purchased fuels
- Upstream emissions associated with purchased electricity
- Transmission and distribution losses
- Certain emissions from the generation of energy purchased by an energy retailer and resold to end users

For an oil and gas operator, this may include emissions from producing and transporting fuels consumed in its own operations, excluding the combustion emissions already reported in Scope 1.

This category is particularly important because the emissions associated with supplying energy can extend well beyond the point at which the energy is consumed.

### Category 4 – Upstream transportation and distribution

Category 4 covers transportation and distribution services purchased by the reporting company, as well as relevant transportation of purchased products between suppliers and the company's operations.

Examples include:

- Transport of raw materials to production facilities
- Inbound logistics
- Warehousing and distribution services purchased by the company
- Transportation between company facilities using third-party providers

For energy-sector organisations, relevant activities may include the transport of equipment, chemicals, steel and other materials to operational sites.

The classification of transportation emissions depends on the activity and the company's role in the transaction. Transportation paid for by the reporting company can fall under Category 4, including certain outbound logistics. Transportation after sale that is not purchased by the reporting company may fall under Category 9.

### Category 5 – Waste generated in operations

This category includes emissions from the treatment and disposal of waste generated in the reporting company's operations during the reporting year, where the treatment takes place in facilities not owned or controlled by the company.

Examples include:

- Landfill disposal
- Wastewater treatment
- Incineration
- Recycling and other waste-treatment processes

Industrial operators may need to consider waste streams from manufacturing, maintenance, construction, drilling and processing activities.

Waste classification and treatment methods matter because different disposal routes can result in different greenhouse gas emissions.

### Category 6 – Business travel

Category 6 covers emissions from employee business travel in vehicles and facilities not owned or operated by the reporting company.

Examples include:

- Air travel
- Rail travel
- Rental cars
- Taxis and other ground transportation
- Hotel stays

For organisations with geographically dispersed operations, business travel can be relevant, particularly where employees regularly travel to remote sites, suppliers or international facilities.

### Category 7 – Employee commuting

This category covers emissions from employees travelling between their homes and their workplaces in vehicles not owned or operated by the reporting company.

Examples include:

- Private cars
- Public transport
- Employee buses
- Motorcycles and other commuting modes

The emissions profile depends on commuting distances, transport modes, occupancy and the location of the workforce.

For companies operating industrial facilities in remote areas, commuting arrangements and workforce transport can be important factors.

### Category 8 – Upstream leased assets

Category 8 covers emissions from the operation of assets leased by the reporting company, where those emissions are not already included in its Scope 1 or Scope 2 inventory.

Examples include:

- Leased buildings
- Leased vehicles
- Leased equipment
- Other assets operated by the company but outside its Scope 1 and Scope 2 boundary

The accounting treatment depends on the company's organisational boundary and how leased assets are treated in its Scope 1 and Scope 2 inventory.

## 3. Downstream Scope 3 emissions: Categories 9–15

Downstream emissions occur after products or services leave the reporting company's operations. They may arise during transportation, further processing, customer use, leasing, franchising or investment activities.

### Category 9 – Downstream transportation and distribution

This category covers transportation and distribution of products sold by the reporting company between its operations and the end consumer, where the transportation and distribution services are not purchased by the reporting company.

Examples include:

- Third-party distribution after sale
- Retail storage and distribution
- Transportation to customers where the customer or another party arranges and pays for the service

For energy companies, the relevant activities may include the distribution of products such as fuels and other energy-related products, depending on the company's role in the supply chain.

### Category 10 – Processing of sold products

Category 10 covers emissions generated when customers or other downstream companies process intermediate products sold by the reporting company.

Examples include:

- Refining or further processing of intermediate materials
- Manufacturing using purchased industrial inputs
- Processing of metals, chemicals or other materials supplied by the reporting company

This category is particularly relevant to companies selling intermediate products that require further processing before they become finished goods.

### Category 11 – Use of sold products

Category 11 includes emissions generated during the use of products sold by the reporting company over their expected lifetime.

Examples include:

- Combustion of sold fuels
- Electricity consumed by sold products
- Fuel consumed by vehicles sold
- Energy used by equipment and appliances sold

For oil and gas companies, the combustion of sold natural gas, petrol, diesel and other fossil fuels can be a major source of downstream Scope 3 emissions.

The scale of these emissions depends on the products sold, their expected use and the applicable calculation assumptions.

It is important to distinguish these emissions from the company's own operational emissions. Combustion of fuel in a company-controlled facility is generally Scope 1; combustion of fuel sold to an external customer is generally Category 11 for the seller.

### Category 12 – End-of-life treatment of sold products

This category covers emissions from the disposal and treatment of products sold by the reporting company at the end of their useful life.

Examples include:

- Landfill disposal
- Incineration
- Recycling
- Wastewater treatment
- Other end-of-life treatment processes

The emissions depend on the product's materials, expected lifetime, disposal route and recovery or recycling assumptions.

### Category 13 – Downstream leased assets

Category 13 covers emissions from the operation of assets owned by the reporting company and leased to other entities, where those emissions are not already included in the company's Scope 1 or Scope 2 inventory.

Examples include:

- Buildings leased to tenants
- Industrial equipment leased to customers
- Vehicles or other assets leased to external users

This category is relevant to companies whose business models include leasing assets to customers or other organisations.

### Category 14 – Franchises

This category covers emissions from the operation of franchises that are not included in the reporting company's Scope 1 or Scope 2 inventory.

Examples include:

- Franchise-operated retail locations
- Franchise-operated service facilities
- Other businesses operating under a franchise agreement

The emissions profile depends on the activities performed by franchise operators and the reporting company's organisational and operational boundaries.

### Category 15 – Investments

Category 15 covers emissions associated with investments made by the reporting company during the reporting year, where those emissions are not already included in Scope 1 or Scope 2.

Examples include:

- Equity investments
- Debt investments
- Project finance
- Other relevant financial investments

This category is particularly important for financial institutions, investment companies and organisations with significant holdings in other businesses or projects.

The accounting of investment-related emissions depends on the applicable asset-class methodology and attribution approach. ## 4. Why the distinction between categories matters

The 15 categories provide a common structure, but applying them requires careful consideration of the company's activities, organisational boundaries and value-chain relationships.

Some activities may appear similar but belong in different categories depending on the circumstances.

For example:

- **Purchased fuel:** emissions from combustion in company-controlled equipment are generally Scope 1, while upstream extraction and supply-chain emissions not included in Scope 1 or 2 are Category 3.
- **Transportation:** purchased transportation services may be Category 4, while certain downstream transportation services not purchased by the reporting company are Category 9.
- **Equipment:** emissions from manufacturing purchased long-term equipment are generally Category 2, while emissions from operating company-controlled equipment are generally Scope 1 or Scope 2.
- **Sold fuels:** emissions from the combustion of fuels sold to customers are generally Category 11, rather than the seller's Scope 1.

Correct classification helps prevent omissions and double counting within a company's inventory. It also improves transparency when emissions data are used for reporting, target-setting and performance analysis.

## 5. Which Scope 3 categories are most relevant to the energy sector?

The relevance of each category depends on the company's business model, operational structure and position in the value chain. There is no single category profile that applies to every energy company.

For example:

| Business activity | Potentially relevant Scope 3 categories |
| --- | --- |
| Oil and gas production | Categories 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 11 |
| Refining and processing | Categories 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 9, 10 and 11 |
| Power generation | Categories 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and potentially 11 |
| Energy equipment manufacturing | Categories 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10 and 11 |
| Energy trading and retail | Categories 1, 3, 4, 9, 11 and potentially 15 |
| Financial institutions financing energy projects | Category 15, using the applicable investment methodology |

These are indicative examples, not definitive category assignments. Actual classification depends on the company's activities, reporting boundary and the specific transaction or value-chain relationship.

For oil and gas companies, Category 11 can be particularly significant because it may include emissions from the use of sold products. Categories 1–4 can also be important due to the materials, equipment, fuels and transportation services required to support operations.

Methane emissions are relevant across several parts of the value chain. Depending on the source and the company's role, methane may be accounted for in Scope 1, Scope 2-related energy accounting or different Scope 3 categories. The correct classification depends on where the emissions occur and the activity generating them.

## 6. How companies can use the 15 categories to build an inventory

The category structure is a starting point for organising data and assigning responsibilities. A practical inventory-development process can follow several steps:

**1. Map the value chain.** Identify key suppliers, purchased goods and services, operational activities, products sold, customers and downstream processes.

**2. Identify relevant categories.** Review all 15 categories and determine which activities occur within the company's value chain.

**3. Establish reporting boundaries.** Define the activities and emissions sources included in each category, taking account of the GHG Protocol's minimum boundaries and any justified exclusions.

**4. Collect activity data.** Gather information such as material quantities, expenditure, fuel consumption, transport distances, waste volumes, product sales and investment data.

**5. Select calculation methods.** Use supplier-specific, activity-based, average-data or spend-based methods as appropriate to the category and data available.

**6. Assess data quality.** Record the origin, coverage, assumptions and uncertainty of the data. Prioritise improvements where emissions are significant or estimates are highly uncertain.

**7. Review classification and double counting.** Check that activities are assigned consistently and that emissions are not counted more than once within the company's inventory.

**8. Update the inventory.** Reassess the categories, data and assumptions periodically as the business, supply chain and reporting requirements change.

Not every company will have significant emissions in all 15 categories. However, categories should be screened systematically, and exclusions should be supported by a clear rationale rather than assumed to be irrelevant.

## 7. From classification to emissions management

Understanding the 15 Scope 3 categories is more than an accounting exercise. It helps organisations identify the activities and relationships that influence their value-chain emissions.

For some companies, the main opportunities may involve supplier engagement and lower-carbon materials. For others, product design, energy efficiency, logistics, customer use or investment decisions may have a greater influence.

The category structure also helps organisations connect emissions data with procurement, operations, product strategy, finance and risk management.

A reliable Scope 3 inventory therefore requires more than a calculation model. It requires a clear understanding of the value chain, consistent classification, traceable data and a process for improving information over time.
