## Introduction

Climate impact is not limited to the emissions released directly from a company's operations. For financial institutions, a substantial part of their climate footprint can be associated with the companies, projects and assets they finance.

A bank may have relatively low direct operational emissions. Its offices, data centres, business travel and company vehicles account for only a fraction of its overall climate exposure. However, the same bank may provide billions of euros in financing to energy companies, industrial facilities, transport operators and infrastructure projects. The emissions associated with these activities can be considerably larger than the emissions generated by the bank's own operations.

This is the starting point for understanding **financed emissions**.

Financed emissions provide a framework for attributing a proportion of the greenhouse gas emissions of borrowers and investee companies to the financial institutions providing capital. They help banks, asset managers, investors and other financial market participants understand the emissions associated with their financing activities and assess their exposure to climate-related transition risks.

For sectors such as oil and gas, where capital-intensive infrastructure and long-lived assets play a central role in business operations, understanding financed emissions is particularly important.

This article explains what financed emissions are, how they are calculated, which methodologies are used, and why reliable emissions data from the companies receiving finance is becoming increasingly important.

## 1. What are financed emissions?

Financed emissions are the greenhouse gas emissions associated with the loans, investments and other financial activities of a financial institution.

They are generally classified under **Scope 3, Category 15 (Investments)** of the Greenhouse Gas Protocol's Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Accounting and Reporting Standard.

Unlike Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions, which relate to an organisation's direct operations and purchased energy, financed emissions reflect the emissions of other entities in which a financial institution has an economic interest or to which it provides financing.

Examples include:

- A commercial bank providing a loan to an oil and gas producer.
- An investment fund holding shares in an electricity generation company.
- A financial institution purchasing corporate bonds issued by a steel manufacturer.
- A bank financing the construction of a natural gas power plant.
- An investor holding equity in a company operating energy infrastructure.

In each case, the financed emissions represent an attributed share of the emissions associated with the underlying borrower, investee or project.

It is important to understand that financed emissions are not necessarily emissions physically generated by the financial institution itself. They are an accounting allocation of the emissions associated with the activities it finances.

This distinction is fundamental. A bank does not operate an oil field simply because it provides financing to its owner. Nevertheless, its lending activity creates a financial relationship with the emissions associated with that operation.

## 2. How do financed emissions relate to Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3?

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol divides corporate greenhouse gas emissions into three scopes.

| Emission category | Description | Example for a financial institution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope 1 | Direct emissions from owned or controlled sources | Fuel combustion in company-owned vehicles |
| Scope 2 | Indirect emissions from purchased electricity, heating and cooling | Electricity consumed in bank offices |
| Scope 3 | Other indirect emissions across the value chain | Emissions associated with loans and investments |

Financed emissions belong to Scope 3, Category 15.

However, an important distinction must be made when reporting the emissions of a financial institution and those of its borrowers.

Consider a bank financing an oil and gas company.

The oil and gas company may report:

- Scope 1 emissions from combustion and methane released through its operations.
- Scope 2 emissions from purchased electricity and energy.
- Scope 3 emissions associated with its wider value chain, including the use of sold products.

The bank then attributes a proportion of these emissions to its financing exposure, according to the applicable accounting methodology.

Consequently, a bank's financed emissions can include attributed Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3 emissions of the companies it finances.

This is why financed emissions should not be confused with the bank's own operational Scope 1, 2 and 3 inventory. They are a specific part of its Scope 3 accounting.

## 3. How are financed emissions calculated?

The central principle behind financed emissions accounting is **attribution**.

A financial institution does not automatically account for 100% of a borrower's emissions. Instead, it calculates an attribution factor that represents its proportional financial exposure to the borrower or investee.

The Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF) provides the principal industry methodology for this process.

The general calculation is:

**Financed emissions = Attribution factor × Company emissions**

At portfolio level, the calculation is repeated for individual borrowers and investee companies, and the resulting emissions are aggregated.

### Calculating the attribution factor

The attribution factor depends on the type of financial exposure.

For business loans to listed companies, the calculation generally uses the outstanding loan amount divided by the company's Enterprise Value Including Cash (EVIC).

For business loans and unlisted equity investments in private companies, the calculation generally uses the outstanding amount divided by the company's total equity and debt.

The general principle can be expressed as:

**Attribution factor = Outstanding financial exposure / Relevant company value**

The applicable denominator must be selected according to the relevant PCAF asset-class methodology.

### Worked example

Consider a bank that provides a business loan to a listed oil and gas company.

Assume the following illustrative figures:

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Outstanding loan | €100 million |
| Company EVIC | €1,000 million |
| Borrower's annual GHG emissions | 1,000,000 tCO₂e |

The attribution factor is:

€100 million / €1,000 million = 10%

The bank's financed emissions are therefore:

10% × 1,000,000 tCO₂e = **100,000 tCO₂e**

The bank would attribute 100,000 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent to this financing exposure.

This is an illustrative calculation. Actual reporting requires the correct asset-class methodology, financial reporting dates, emissions boundaries and data-quality considerations.

It also demonstrates why a company's financial valuation and capital structure can influence the amount of emissions attributed to a particular financial institution.

## 4. What is PCAF and why is it important?

The Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF) is an industry-led initiative that has developed a standardised approach to measuring and reporting financed emissions.

Its objective is to improve consistency, transparency and comparability in greenhouse gas accounting across financial institutions.

The Global GHG Accounting and Reporting Standard for the Financial Industry provides calculation methodologies for different financial asset classes.

These include:

- Listed equity and corporate bonds.
- Business loans and unlisted equity.
- Project finance.
- Commercial real estate.
- Mortgages.
- Motor vehicle loans.
- Sovereign debt.

The methodologies recognise that different types of financial exposure require different approaches to emissions attribution.

For example, financing a listed company through a corporate bond is not necessarily accounted for in the same way as financing a specific industrial project.

The third edition of PCAF's Part A standard, published in 2025, further develops the methodology and expands the available guidance.

PCAF is particularly relevant because it provides a common accounting framework that enables financial institutions to build portfolio-level emissions inventories rather than relying solely on individual company disclosures.

However, applying a standard methodology does not automatically guarantee accurate results. The quality of the calculation still depends heavily on the quality and completeness of the underlying emissions and financial data.

## 5. Why does emissions data quality matter?

Financed emissions accounting is only as reliable as the information used in the calculation.

Financial institutions often have access to financial information about their borrowers, including loan amounts, outstanding balances and company valuations. Obtaining accurate and comparable greenhouse gas emissions data can be considerably more difficult.

In practice, emissions data may come from several sources:

1. Reported emissions: Data published directly by the borrower or investee company, ideally verified by an independent third party.
2. Physical activity-based estimates: Calculations based on operational data, such as fuel consumption, electricity use or industrial production volumes.
3. Economic activity-based estimates: Calculations using financial information and sector-specific emissions intensities.

PCAF recognises different data-quality levels, with Score 1 representing the highest data quality and Score 5 the lowest.

The distinction is important. A financed emissions figure based on verified, company-reported emissions is not equivalent in reliability to one calculated using sector averages and financial estimates.

Both may be useful for portfolio accounting, but they communicate different levels of confidence.

For financial institutions, improving data quality should therefore be an ongoing process rather than a one-time reporting exercise.

### The connection with methane emissions

This issue becomes particularly important when financing companies in the oil and gas sector.

Methane emissions are a good example of why conventional corporate reporting data may not always provide a sufficiently detailed picture of environmental performance.

An oil and gas company may report its total greenhouse gas emissions, but the quality of the underlying methane information can vary considerably depending on its measurement methods, emission factors, operational coverage and verification practices.

For example, methane emissions estimated using generic emission factors may differ significantly from results obtained through source-level measurement and site-level reconciliation.

Frameworks such as the Oil and Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 (OGMP 2.0) are relevant because they promote more detailed measurement, reporting and reconciliation of methane emissions.

For a financial institution, access to more accurate methane data can improve the assessment of the financed company's emissions profile and provide a better basis for understanding its operational and regulatory exposure.

The relationship between emissions measurement and financial accounting is therefore direct: better operational data supports better financed emissions accounting.
