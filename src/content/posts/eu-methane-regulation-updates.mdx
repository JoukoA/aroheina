Today, the European Parliament endorsed a deal with member states to postpone key methane monitoring and reporting obligations for importers of oil, gas and coal. The headline is “delay”. The more useful reading is “sequencing”: the world’s first comprehensive methane law, which also reaches imports, has not been repealed or diluted in principle. Its hardest commercial test has been moved back.

## What was actually decided

The Commission proposed postponing certain importer obligations, originally due from 1 January 2027, along with methane intensity reporting, originally due by August 2028. The trigger was warnings from importers that they might not secure enough compliant supply in a strained market, with prices pushed up by the conflict in the Middle East.

Negotiators from Parliament and Council agreed on 19 November that a postponement was needed, but opted for a longer, two-year delay. Under the deal, importers will have until May 2030 to demonstrate that supply contracts concluded or renewed since 4 August 2024 cover only fuel subject to monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) measures equivalent to EU rules.

## The politics: a comfortable majority, not a consensus

Parliament handled the file under its urgent procedure. Plenary confirmed the agreement with 341 votes in favour, 224 against and 31 abstentions. The Council still has to formally endorse it before publication in the Official Journal, so the text is not yet law.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/20261126-202917.png" alt=""/><figcaption>Figure 2. Plenary vote on the methane deal, 26 November 2026. Percentages are of the 596 votes cast (rounded).</figcaption></figure>

The vote shows a real split. Nearly four in ten MEPs opposed any softening of a flagship climate law.

## Why this matters beyond the headline

Three points stand out for anyone running assets, trading molecules or financing them.

**1. The contract clock is unchanged.** The relevant contracts are those concluded or renewed since 4 August 2024. Every such deal signed today is already a bet on whether the supplier can evidence equivalent MRV by 2030. Buyers who wait to write measurement clauses will find the market has moved without them.

**2. Equivalence is an engineering problem, not a paperwork problem.** Proving that a third-country supply chain meets standards equivalent to EU rules means credible source-level measurement, reconciliation of bottom-up inventories with top-down data, and independent verification. Frameworks such as OGMP 2.0 and third-party certification like MiQ are the practical bridge. Two extra years is a window to build that evidence trail, not a reason to defer it.

**3. Regulatory certainty has become a cost of capital issue.** A deadline that moves once can move again, and lenders and investors know it. Financed-emissions exposure and the valuation of gas assets increasingly depend on measured intensity, not on declared intent. Suppliers with verified low-methane volumes will carry a commercial advantage whichever year the rule bites.

## What to do with the extra time

**A pragmatic checklist**

- Inventory every supply contract signed or renewed since 4 August 2024 and flag its MRV status.
- Write measurement, reporting and verification rights into new and renewed contracts now.
- Treat equivalence as a data-quality ladder, and set a target rung per supplier and per year.
- Budget for verification and certification as a recurring cost, not a one-off.
- Watch the Council endorsement and the final text for the exact intensity-reporting date.

## The bottom line

The delay answers a real supply-security worry in a tight market. It does not answer the underlying question of how to prove what is leaving a pipe, a flare or a well. Companies that use the pause to build measurement credibility will be selling a differentiated product in 2030. Those that treat it as a reprieve may discover that buyers, banks and regulators kept the old calendar in their heads.
