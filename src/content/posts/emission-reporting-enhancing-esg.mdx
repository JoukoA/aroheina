ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) has moved from a niche concept to a mainstream driver of investment and business strategy. For energy companies, methane emissions are a critical and highly scrutinized component of the "E" in ESG. While many companies report their emissions, a reliance on estimated data is a fundamental flaw that can undermine trust and expose a company to significant reputational risk. The key to building genuine trust is to move from estimation to transparent, measurement-based reporting.

## Understanding Emissions: The Three Scopes

To build a credible ESG profile, it is essential to first understand where your emissions originate. The Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol categorizes a company's emissions into three distinct scopes:

**Scope 1:** Direct Emissions. These are emissions from sources that are owned or controlled by your company. For an oil and gas company, this includes fugitive emissions from leaks, flaring, venting, and combustion from on-site generators or company vehicles. These are the emissions you have direct control over, making them the most critical to accurately measure and report.

**Scope 2:** Indirect Emissions from Purchased Energy. These are emissions generated off-site from the production of electricity, steam, or heat that your company purchases and uses.

**Scope 3:** Other Indirect Value Chain Emissions. This is a broad category that includes all other emissions, both upstream and downstream, that occur as a result of your business's activities. Examples include emissions from the production of raw materials, business travel, or the end-use of products you sell to customers.

While Scope 3 emissions can often be the largest, a strong ESG foundation begins with a focus on Scope 1 emissions, where direct measurement and reduction are possible.

## The Flaw in the System: The Problem with Estimated Data

For years, emissions reporting has been based largely on models and estimations. These methods, while convenient, are not always accurate and are increasingly viewed with skepticism by investors, regulators, and the public. This lack of verifiable data creates a trust deficit, as stakeholders cannot be sure that a company is truly making progress on its environmental commitments. This gap between what is reported and what is actually happening is a significant reputational liability.

## From Estimation to Verification: The Power of Measurement

Transparent emissions reporting is built on a foundation of verified, measurement-based data. Standards like OGMP 2.0 formalize this shift, requiring companies to move beyond Tier 1 and 2 estimations to a Gold Standard of reporting based on direct measurements. This approach provides auditable data that gives stakeholders confidence in your environmental performance. It is a proactive step that shows you have nothing to hide and are committed to real, demonstrable progress.

## The Business Benefits of a Strong ESG Profile

A high-quality ESG profile, underpinned by transparent emissions reporting, can lead to several powerful business advantages:

**Attracting Capital:** A strong ESG score can attract environmentally conscious investors and lead to a lower cost of capital, providing a significant competitive edge.

**Competitive Advantage:** As stakeholders demand more transparency, companies with a verifiable record of emissions reduction will be favored. This can lead to increased market access and a stronger brand reputation.

**Enhanced Reputation:** By actively and transparently managing emissions, you demonstrate your company's commitment to environmental stewardship. This builds public trust and strengthens your license to operate.
